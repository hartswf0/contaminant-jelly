## A · Thesis, defining passage, the forgettable version

### Thesis (three sentences)
A dog that cannot be ordered chooses, again and again, to go under the water for someone who cannot,
and every time it comes back up there is a real thing in its mouth. The film is about what happens
after "no": the dog does not argue, it goes down again a different way, and this time somebody who
built nothing is watching what comes up. A child should leave able to say: "you ask, it decides to go,
it digs and sniffs and carries, it calls other dogs when the job is too big, and when you say no it
goes back and does it better."

### Formal choices that give it its identity
1. **One line, two worlds.** The surface is a bright line that is in every shot. Amber and violet above
   it, teal below it. Every crossing is a shot of its own and costs the dog something (breath, a shake,
   wet paper).
2. **Everything that goes down comes up with something.** No dog ever surfaces empty-mouthed, except
   the eleven in S5. That one exception is the plot.
3. **Thinking is a hold.** The dog looks, holds still, then moves. The hold is always there before a
   choice and never there for an order. Holds are listed with their seconds in part C.
4. **Every dog is made a different way** (paper, black cut-out, wire, wooden toy, crepe, print), so
   a pack reads as many makers, never as copies.
5. **The drawing itself stops breathing under the ice.** For those seconds the line boil is switched
   off. Nothing else in the film is ever that still.

### The defining passage (S6, 161.0 to 173.0, twelve seconds)
A wooden retriever comes up with a reel and is proud of it. The old dog, lying across the sill, looks
at it for a second and a half, gets up slowly, turns round, and lies down again with its back to the
reel. Nobody speaks. The builder looks at the old dog's back, looks at the person (no help there),
lowers its ears, and goes down again. It comes back with the reel changed. The old dog looks over its
shoulder, then shifts its hindquarters off the sill to make room. The reel is laid down.

Everything before it exists to earn it:
- S1 teaches that a dog's back and a dog's hold mean something (the dog backs off from the hand).
- S2 teaches what "right" looks like: the fish child's own wobbly drawing in a bubble.
- S5 removes the lookers, so a wrong reel gets through; S6 opens on the cost of that.
- The old dog has been on the sill since 95.0 doing nothing at all, so that its one move is the
  biggest move in the film.

Build this passage FIRST (law 3), with the lead dog's ears-down beat (149.0 to 153.0) attached, before
anything else is multiplied.

### What would make it competent but forgettable
- Dogs that glide. If digging does not throw gravel, if a shake does not throw water, if a wet page
  does not sag, it is clip-art on a path.
- The old dog's turn played fast, or played as a head shake, or explained by a symbol (a cross, a red
  mark, a thought bubble). It must be a slow whole-body turn and nothing else.
- The "no" played as scolding and the dog played as sad-then-happy. The dog is not punished. It is
  told, it takes it in, it goes.
- Even intensity: every beat the same length, every dog always moving. The film needs three places
  where almost nothing moves (18.5, 118 to 123, 164.0 to 165.5).
- A pack that arrives in single file and works one at a time. Rejected already in the history.
- Anything that looks like an interface: boxes with rounded corners, rows, arrows, ticks, counters,
  a label on a dog, lettering on a building. Rejected already, three times.
- Copies: nine dogs that are the lead dog in different colours.
- Music laid under everything to tell us what to feel.

## B · The bar: five tests a stranger applies with the sound off

Each is a yes or no. A critic who built nothing shows the film muted to someone who has not read this
board (or acts as that person with fresh context) and records the answers. Classified JUDGED unless
marked otherwise.

1. **The retelling test.** After one viewing the stranger says, unprompted, in their own words: the
   person asked; the dog decided to go; it dug, sniffed and carried; it called other dogs; the person
   said no; the dog went back; an old dog turned away from one and it was done again. Seven facts.
   Six or more is a pass.
2. **The silhouette test.** Pause on any ten frames chosen by the critic (at least four of them pack
   shots). Fill every character with flat black. The stranger can still point to the lead dog (torn
   ear, collar notch) in every frame where it is present, and can tell the old dog from the others.
3. **The comes-back test (MEASURED by the critic, counted from the picture).** For every time any dog
   crosses down through the surface on screen, there is a later frame of that dog (or its group)
   crossing up with a visible thing in its mouth. The only exceptions are the eleven in S5, and the
   stranger names them as "the ones who came up with nothing".
4. **The why-no test.** Pause at 148.0. Ask "is the person going to like it, and why or why not?"
   A pass is any answer of the kind "no, it's too neat / it isn't how the fish kid drew it". Then
   pause at 165.5 and ask "what is the old dog saying?" A pass is "no" or "not good enough" with no
   symbol on screen to say it.
5. **The nothing-to-read test (MEASURED).** Step through at one frame per second (180 frames). Count
   lettered words: the title and the end credit only, 14 words. Count rectangles of interface, cards,
   charts, digits, arrows, ticks, labels: zero. Lettering on signs, the radio, the board, the reels:
   none (marks and scribble only).

Comparison material that can actually be opened (not yet inspected by the director, so UNKNOWN as a
bar until a critic looks): `assets/films/cowpath-study-refuse.mp4` for a refusal carried by a body,
`assets/films/cowpath-study-shadow.mp4` for light on a made figure. Compare acting with acting only.
No external reference was available to inspect; the five tests above are the bar, and that limitation
is recorded here.

small pushes. Left is always the door; right and down is always the water.

### Light
Above: violet sky (darkening by about a fifth from S1 to S6; one star at 127, a few by 173), amber
lamp is the only warm source, everything away from it is a violet silhouette. Below: teal, lighter
near the surface, lamps of Trouttown small and warm. The lit door is visible from below as an amber
smear at the upper left of the surface in every F-TOWN and F-SPLIT shot. That smear is what the fish
children look up at in the last shot, so it must be there from S2 on.

## F · Sound plan, beat by beat

Principles. Two rooms: ABOVE (dry, close, small sounds, crickets, lamp buzz) and BELOW (everything
through a low-pass at about 700 Hz, slow, a low water drone). The crossing is the swap between them
and is the film's signature sound. No continuous music. The radio is the only music and it is thin,
plucked, and comes from one place (pan left of centre). Silence is used three times: 18.5, 118 to
123, 164.0 to 165.5. All of it is synthesized with `kit.Mix`; names below are Mix methods. Sound
perspective follows the framing: in F-SPLIT both rooms at half level.
