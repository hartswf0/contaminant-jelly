The lesson: how language models make films with SCION prompts, the porthole game, and the idea that nobody is holding the chalk.
Written through three operators from the worldtext prompt page: Pitch Architect (T031), Operational Pragmatist (T020), Structural Syntactician (T005).

## Session Title
Nobody Is Holding the Chalk: How Fourth Graders' Worksheets Direct AI Films

## Session Synopsis
Scissors cut a boot from an old poster. Chalk writes on a board. No hand holds either one. A language model made this film, and nobody is holding the chalk. So who decides what gets drawn? Whoever writes the rules. This session shows the rules. A SCION prompt gives a crew of AI agents four things: a claim to prove, one hard scene that proves it, a job for each agent, and a judge who built nothing. The agents write drawing code and cut paper from credited archives. They never guess pixels. Then fourth graders take the chalk. In the porthole game, one student draws what is outside a turkey's submarine and passes the page left. Two classmates write what Turkey thinks and what the trout think. The AI crew must film each sentence as written and leave each blank line blank. You will play a round, watch a child's page become a film, and take home the game, the films, and the prompts. STEAM Forward: Inspiring Innovation, Impact, and Possibility starts when students write the rules.

## Session Engagement
The session has three parts, and the room works in all three.

Watch. We play one minute of film. Scissors, pins, a pen, and chalk move with no hand on them. We ask one question: who is holding the chalk? People answer out loud. Every answer goes on the board.

Play. You sit in a group of three with one printed page. The first person draws what is outside the porthole. The page moves left. The second person writes for Turkey, inside the submarine. The third person writes for the trout, outside. Turkey and the trout read the same thing in two ways, and both readings count. The game runs on disagreement.

Direct. You see a fourth grader's page and the film an AI crew made from it. You read the four parts of the SCION prompt that the crew followed. Then your group names one rule it would add.

A classroom teacher and a university researcher present together, so the session speaks from the classroom and from the lab. You need no drawing skill, no AI account, and no device. You can draw, write, or tell a partner what to write. We read every instruction aloud and print it in large type. The films have captions and narration. Three classroom rules apply to adults too: blank is okay, wild is good, being stuck is okay too.

## Takeaways
1. The porthole game, ready to run. One printed page, three seats, one pass to the left, three kinds of ending, and one die. You leave with the page, the numbered slips for all 55 portholes, and a six-minute film that teaches the steps to a class. One fourth-grade class has played it. Five of their pages became films.

2. A SCION prompt you can read and reuse. It has four parts: a claim to prove, one hard scene that proves it, a job for each maker, and a judge who built nothing. You see the prompts that made these films. You leave with a one-page version that students can fill in for any project, with or without AI.

3. One question for every AI picture, film, or essay: who is holding the chalk? Students answer it by finding the rules. What did the rules keep? What did the machine add? Who decided? In these films the answers are public. Every sentence on screen is a child's sentence. Every blank line stays blank. Every picture is drawn in code or cut from a credited archive.
