## Session Title
Nobody Is Holding the Chalk: How Fourth Graders' Worksheets Direct AI Films

## Session Synopsis (970 characters)
Most AI video guesses pixels from a short caption. This session shows another way: language models that make films by writing drawing code and cutting credited archival and handmade paper, under rules people set. In Tourkey Trouttown, a fourth-grade class plays a one-page porthole game. A pilot draws what is outside a turkey's submarine and passes the page left; two friends write what Turkey thinks and what the trout think, from probable to preposterous. The handwritten sheets then act as contracts for a crew of AI agents (SCION): every sentence stays the child's, every blank stays blank, and an independent critic checks the film against the page. Participants play a round, watch a page become a film, and audit one themselves. STEAM Forward: Inspiring Innovation, Impact, and Possibility: students move from pulling a slot machine's lever to writing the rules a machine must follow, with three permissions: blank is okay, wild is good, being stuck is okay too.

## Session Engagement
Everyone plays, and everyone judges. Participants sit in threes with one printed page and take the three seats in turn: the pilot draws, the page is passed left, the next person writes for Turkey inside the sub, the last writes for the trout outside. The game is built on two readings of one thing (Turkey thinks, the trout think), so disagreement is the material, not a problem to settle.

Each group then becomes the independent critic. They get a class page and the film made from it and mark three things: what the film kept, what the film added, and what they cannot tell. Builders never grade their own work in this method, and neither do participants: groups swap pages before judging.

Nobody needs to draw well, use AI, or bring a device. People may draw, write, or dictate to a partner. Instructions are read aloud and printed large; the films are captioned and narrated; sharing with the room is voluntary. The three permissions from the classroom apply in the session too: blank is okay, wild is good, being stuck is okay too.

## Takeaways
1. A one-page game that turns students into authors of rules. One sheet, three seats, a pass to the left, three kinds of ending (probable, plausible, preposterous) and one die. Attendees leave with the sheet, the numbered slips for all 55 portholes, and a six-minute film that teaches the steps to a class.

2. A contract for AI work on student writing. Four clauses attendees can use with any tool: the student's sentence is never changed; a blank stays blank; nothing is faked (every picture is handmade in code or taken from a credited open archive); and the maker never awards its own pass (someone who did not build the film checks it against the page). Attendees practice by auditing one film.

3. A way to talk with students about how AI films get made. Attendees see the difference between a video model that guesses pixels and language models that write drawing code under stated rules, and get a short vocabulary for claims: measured, checked by structure, judged by a viewer, or unknown. They can use it to ask of any AI output: what was kept, what was added, and who decided?
