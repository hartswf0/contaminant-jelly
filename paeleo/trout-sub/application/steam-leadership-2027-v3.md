## Session Title
Nobody Is Holding the Chalk: How Fourth Graders' Worksheets Direct AI Films

## Session Synopsis
Most AI video tools guess pixels from a short caption. This session shows another method. Language models write drawing code and cut paper from credited archives, and people set the rules. In Tourkey Trouttown, a fourth-grade class plays a one-page game. One student draws what is outside a turkey's submarine. The page moves left. Two classmates write what Turkey thinks and what the trout think. Each writes three endings: probable, plausible, preposterous. A crew of AI agents, called SCION, then makes a film from the page. The crew keeps every sentence as the child wrote it. It leaves every blank line blank. A separate critic checks the film against the page. In this session you play one round, watch one page become a film, and check one film yourself. The session answers the theme STEAM Forward: Inspiring Innovation, Impact, and Possibility. Students write the rules that the machine must follow. Three rules protect them: blank is okay, wild is good, being stuck is okay too.

## Session Engagement
Everyone plays. Then everyone judges.

Play. You sit in a group of three with one printed page. The first person draws what is outside the porthole. The page moves left. The second person writes for Turkey, inside the submarine. The third person writes for the trout, outside. Turkey and the trout read the same thing in two ways, and both readings count. The game needs people to disagree.

Judge. Your group swaps pages with another group. You receive a class page and the film made from it. You mark three things: what the film kept, what the film added, and what you cannot tell. Nobody judges their own work.

You do not need drawing skill, an AI account, or a device. You can draw, write, or tell a partner what to write. A presenter reads every instruction aloud, and every instruction is printed in large type. The films have captions and narration. You share with the room only if you choose to. The three classroom rules apply here: blank is okay, wild is good, being stuck is okay too.

## Takeaways
1. A one-page game where students write the rules. You get one sheet, three seats, one pass to the left, three kinds of ending, and one die. You leave with the sheet, the numbered slips for all 55 portholes, and a six-minute film that teaches the steps to a class.

2. A four-rule contract for AI work on student writing. Rule one: never change the student's sentence. Rule two: leave a blank line blank. Rule three: fake nothing. Every picture is drawn in code or taken from a credited open archive. Rule four: the maker never approves its own work. A person or agent who did not build the film checks it against the page. You practice the contract on one film.

3. Four labels for any AI claim. Label each claim measured, checked by structure, judged by a viewer, or unknown. You also see two ways an AI makes a film. A video model guesses pixels. A language model writes drawing code under stated rules. Use both with students to ask three questions of any AI output. What did it keep? What did it add? Who decided?
