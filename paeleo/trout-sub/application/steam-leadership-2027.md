## Session Title
Games That Make Films, Films That Run Games: Tourkey Trouttown

## Session Synopsis (949 characters)
Tourkey Trouttown is a paper game that makes films, and a film that runs the game. Each child gets one of 55 portholes on a turkey's submarine. The pilot draws what is outside. The page is passed, and two friends write three endings each: what Turkey thinks, then what the trout think, from probable to preposterous. A fourth-grade class played it. We turned five of their pages into short animated films in which every sentence on screen is a child's own and every blank stays blank, then into films of the writing itself: the passing, the picturing, the getting stuck. Participants will play one round, watch a class page become a film, and check the film against the page: what was kept, what was added, who decided. STEAM Forward: Inspiring Innovation, Impact, and Possibility: the session shows AI used as a production crew that works under rules the children set, and gives teachers a one-page game for taking another creature's point of view.

## Session Engagement
Everyone plays. Participants sit in threes with one printed page: one person is the pilot and draws, the page is passed, the second writes for Turkey inside the sub, the third writes for the trout outside. Nobody needs to draw well, use AI, or bring a device. People may draw, write, or tell a partner what to write, and leaving a line blank is allowed; in the class's own pages the blanks turned out to matter, and the films keep them.

The game is built so that different readings are the point: the same thing outside the glass is a dinner bell to one player and a prank to another, and both are right. After playing, each group compares its page with a class page and its film and says what the film kept, what it added, and what they would have decided differently. Instructions are read aloud and printed large, the films are captioned and narrated, and sharing with the room is voluntary. The game was first made for fourth graders, including students with IEPs, so each step is one short instruction.

## Takeaways
1. A one-page perspective-taking game they can run on Monday. One printed sheet, three roles (pilot, Turkey, trout), three kinds of ending (probable, plausible, preposterous), and a die. Attendees leave with the sheet, the numbered slips for all 55 portholes, and a 6-minute film that teaches the steps to a class.

2. A rule set for using AI on children's work without overwriting it. The films were made under four rules attendees can reuse with any tool: the child's sentence is never changed; a blank stays blank; children appear by first initial only and their pages are never shown; every film is published with its transcript, sources, and the instructions that made it. Attendees practice applying the rules by checking one film against its page.

3. A way to show thinking, not only answers. A second set of films shows the children working: the pencil hovering, a line rubbed out, a spelling by ear, a friend who did not write. Each struggle shown is one the page records. Attendees see how the traces on a worksheet (not the finished answer) can be read, discussed with students, and used to plan the next round.
